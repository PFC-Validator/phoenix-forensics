How Philipp consolidated power and resources

Record prepared 24 October 2026Sources & methodSupporting sources
012022–2023

Community funding, fee income and control over pooled stake

Community money financed ERIS development. Users supplied the stake behind its delegation and governance influence.

After the rejected 450,000-LUNA request, the revised grant requested 312,500 LUNA to finish the ERIS product portfolio. Its stated budget was $350,000 for salaries, $100,000 for audits, $35,000 for marketing and $15,000 for infrastructure. The grant described a delegation and governance framework among the products the community was funding.

On 20 January 2023, the 312,500 LUNA reached the grant’s 4-of-5 community multisig, worth $598,925.52 at receipt. The funding terms provided for staged release and vesting against milestones. The accompanying ERIS grant material also published a 10% community-revenue commitment. This was a funded commitment to deliver infrastructure for Terra.

Liquid staking added a second source of power: users’ pooled stake. The ERIS hub holds the native delegation. Its voting operator can cast the hub’s governance ballot, while its allocation decisions affect validators’ stake and commission income. Users retain the economic claim through the derivative token; the hub concentrates the native delegation and its voting route.

By the 24 October 2026 snapshot, the hub delegated 31,977,210.37 LUNA — approximately $1,344,261.58 at that snapshot’s historical reference price. The dated control map below identifies Philipp’s owner permission to change the voting operator and his separate administration over Prop Gauge. The combined position links a fee-generating business to voting weight and validator dependence.

The control is exercised over other people’s capital. The deposits make the hub a large native delegator; the owner decides eligible validators and can replace the voting operator. That gives the operator two different forms of leverage: a route to cast the pooled stake’s chain-governance vote, and discretion over which validators receive the delegation and its commission income.

Consequence

Community funding supported development; users’ deposits supplied the principal. The operating structure concentrated fees, delegation decisions and the route through which that pooled stake votes.

The grant receipt, milestone terms, published revenue commitment and 24 October 2026 control snapshot have separate source records and dates.

02July 2023

Public liquidity deepened the market for ERIS products

The community supplied capital as well as a development grant.

Native proposal 4736, submitted on 3 July 2023 and passed, proposed 5 million LUNA for ampLUNA–LUNA and bLUNA–LUNA liquidity: 2.5 million LUNA per liquid-staking product. The instruction amount was worth $3,264,957.03 at submission. The revised proposal explicitly replaced the earlier rejected submission.

For ERIS, deeper ampLUNA liquidity makes its liquid-staking product easier to enter and exit. More use of that product can enlarge the native stake pooled under its operating structure. The grant funded the product; public liquidity supported its market; deposits supplied the stake behind its voting and delegation power.

Consequence

Public capital supported infrastructure through which ERIS could earn fees and aggregate stake. The development award and the liquidity allocation served different parts of the same operating position.

03June–October 2024

Paid to build TLA, while keeping the keys

Philipp became both a funded developer and the holder of powers that could change the system the board was supposed to oversee.

The TLA development and audit award paid 384,615 LUNA on 7 June 2024, worth $250,986.08 at receipt. On 9 August, the original ten TLA contracts were deployed with Philipp’s configuration wallet as administrator.

His 12 August 2024 launch proposal stated: “Contract Admin will be transferred to PD Stewardship after 1 month.” It separately stated: “Ownership transferred to PD Stewardship after 3 months.” Those terms described a time-limited developer position followed by institutional control.

On 2 September, administration of all ten contracts moved to Philipp’s wallet. Authority moved between his wallets; Stewardship did not receive it. In the 18 October 2026 inventory, the original ten contracts still named his administrator wallet.

The Treasury shows a separate, equally concrete sequence. On 7 October 2024, its configuration role was assigned to the board. On 8 October, Philipp’s configuration wallet reassigned that role to itself. Normal board voting therefore coexisted with authority held outside that voting process.

The crucial retained power was the ability to replace contract code. Administration determines who can change the implementation; ownership determines who can exercise the contract’s privileged functions. A board may control routine proposals while another wallet can change the rules under which those proposals operate.

Consequence

The published arrangement required a handover. The recorded result left Philipp with technical powers outside ordinary collective approval, including the power to change the software enforcing that approval.

Record details

The original ten-contract TLA commitment and the later Treasury are traced separately. The strict post-handover count uses 27 November 2024 as its conservative cutoff.

04August–October 2024 onward

PD added recurring inflation income and a second delegation system

Phoenix funding and TLA allocations gave the board influence over both projects and validators.

Proposal 4816 established the Phoenix Directive framework. 4817 introduced three TLA buckets with target reward weights of 10%, 5% and 5%. 4822 introduced a separate Phoenix Treasury Alliance weight of 14%, described in the proposal as allocating 10% of chain staking rewards to PD. 4823 replaced earlier chain/ampROAR allocations with a 5% single-asset bucket.

These are the origins of recurring resource-allocation power: who gets funded, which liquidity earns incentives, and which validators receive the Treasury’s and connectors’ synthetic Alliance delegations. The percentages are the proposal’s specified weights and descriptions, with different activation schedules; they are not amounts to add into a present-day personal ownership share.

ERIS’s pooled-stake voting route was used in that reform. On 10 October 2024 at 10:39:31 UTC, Philipp’s fee account submitted a YES instruction to Prop Gauge for proposal 4823. The successful transaction explicitly records the ERIS hub as voter with 100% YES. This connects the liquid-staking governance channel to the decision replacing earlier allocations with the new single-asset system.

The board used that allocation power. A4–A6, A20 and A21 set up virtual-token staking. Later redelegations include A61, A81–A82, A114–A115, A136, A194–A198 and A218. A114 directed additional allocations to named validators; A115 explicitly removed all allocations from Strangelove.

By 22 October 2026, the saved Openfields model estimated 94.75 million LUNA-equivalent of active synthetic staking weight directed through the Treasury and four TLA holders—about 31.7% of its bonded-weight denominator. Several validators depended on these allocations for most of their recorded weight. This is a second channel alongside ERIS’s native LUNA pool.

Ordinary delegation decisions belonged to the board. The power to replace that delegation-controller role remained with Philipp’s registry-owner wallet. His separate administrator wallet could replace the registry’s code. The board-controlled allocation layer therefore sat beneath a retained technical authority.

Consequence

The same institutional structure could distribute grants and incentives, allocate validator weight, and sustain its own recurring funding. Philipp participated in its collective decisions while retaining separate authority over the implementation.

Record details

The synthetic amount is Alliance staking weight, not 94.75 million withdrawable LUNA. The validator table below preserves native and synthetic allocations in separate columns.

05November 2024–March 2025

Approving benefits for directors and setting conditions for others

Personal allocations, paid governance roles and control over participation developed within the same decision structure.

A43, proposed by Philipp on 5 November 2024, created four permanent locks of 20,750 ampLUNA each for stewardship members. Philipp and MB both voted YES. Philipp’s own allocation was worth $9,338.13 at receipt. A49, proposed by Philipp on 9 November, authorized payments for setting up PD, including 10,000 USDC to his wallet. Philipp and MB again voted YES.

These were beneficiary approvals: people receiving allocations also participated in authorizing them. The transactions identify the recipients and the ballots identify the approvers. The conflict is built into the decision, so the relevant records are the entitlement, disclosure and independent review for each payment.

After the handover period, Philipp’s retained owner authority was used on 18 December 2024 to admit ERIS arbLUNA to voting escrow. Later migrations changed the gauge and incentive manager. The developer retained the means to alter participation while his own products operated inside the system.

The asymmetry matters: the same governance network approved benefits for insiders while setting the requirements other participants had to satisfy to keep economic support.

Consequence

Board position carried financial benefits and influence over eligibility. Participants seeking incentives depended on decisions from people who also held funded roles and product interests.

Record details

The WHALE suspension received a native community vote. The register preserves that authorization and the later implementation alongside the beneficiary allocations.

The same people approved payments to themselves

Commercial beneficiaries, governance allies and technical administrators overlapped in identifiable payment decisions.

On 4 August 2025, Philipp proposed Superbolt P35, “Distribute Treasury”, described as payment of open invoices. He voted YES on his own proposal. The three YES voters were Philipp, MB and Rebel; each was also a recipient in the distribution.

MB executed the payment on 5 August. It included 45,000 USDC to Philipp — $44,994.60 at execution — and three allocations of 15,000 USDC, approximately $14,998.20 each. On 1 October 2025, Philipp’s retained TLA registry-owner authority changed the incentive-creation fee recipient from Stewardship to the ERIS fee account. That is a recorded use of retained authority to change who receives a revenue stream. The reconciled stream contains 2,280 LUNA across 228 receipts, worth $159.95 at receipt-time prices.

The financial record also contains separately priced stewardship compensation, project grants, locked capital, invoice payments and protocol fees. Payments, ballots and retained keys belong in the same account of control. The people benefiting from funded work also participated in approvals, and Philipp retained a separate technical route for changing financial destinations.

The commercial relationship is recorded in transactions. It must be assessed alongside the pair’s governance alignment and the administrator’s power to alter payment rules.

09September–October 2026

Retained powers were used to restrict participation

Code changes, blacklists and delegation decisions turned control into concrete consequences for users, assets and validators.

On 22 September 2026, migrations added asset-level incentive exclusion. The owner used the new list and changed the destination for excluded rewards to a PF operations wallet. Some broad exclusions were reversed later that afternoon. On 23 September, Treasury migration administration moved between Philipp’s two wallets.

On 3 October, code 4037 and then 4038 introduced sender-specific restrictions on staking, unstaking and reward claiming. The configured address remained listed until 18 October. Controlled execution of the deployed binary confirms those restrictions; the installation and add/remove transactions are preserved.

These were changes to what participants could do with the system. Removing a particular blacklist entry leaves the administrator’s ability to install or alter such rules in place. Restoring access for an address and surrendering the power to restrict access are different actions.

The separate ERIS delegation route was also exercised. On 9 October, Philipp’s owner wallet removed Ceres from the eligible validator list and rebalanced delegations. Those transactions show how operator discretion can change a validator’s allocation and associated income.

Across the strict original-TLA subset, the preserved record contains 31 operations in 19 successful transactions on seven contracts after the handover cutoff. This includes migrations, registry changes and participation restrictions. The retained powers were repeatedly used.

Consequence

Philipp could influence the rules of participation through retained TLA authority and validator allocation through the separate ERIS owner role. Both created practical dependence on decisions he could make.

103–24 October 2026

A272 would have made Philipp and MB sufficient authority alone

The proposed removal also changed the approval rule, eliminating the need for consent from anyone outside the pair.

Philipp’s A272, submitted on 3 October 2026, combined Vini’s removal with a threshold change from 75% to 60%. With four equal seats and a 75% threshold, Philipp and MB need a third vote. With three equal seats and a 60% threshold, their two votes are sufficient even when the third director votes NO.

Philipp and MB both voted YES. A272 failed. The attempted change is recorded in executable instructions, not inferred from rhetoric. It would have made an independent director’s agreement dispensable while leaving that person nominally on the board.

The pair’s existing relationship is relevant to that effect. In the A1–A289 record, MB voted YES on 251 of the 252 Philipp proposals on which he voted; one ballot was NO. The one NO was on A28, which used the wrong contract; MB voted YES on corrected A29 5 minutes 10 seconds later. They also participated together in commercial distributions. Their proposed sufficiency must therefore be assessed as the sufficiency of a financially aligned voting bloc.

The later Emergency Supervisor thread continued the removal process. In post #5, Philipp explicitly linked removal to two-of-three. In post #57, he offered a temporary undertaking to vote only NO or ABSTAIN, with an exception for onboarding directors. That undertaking concerns his intended use of authority; it leaves the voting permissions in place and makes an exception for choosing the future board.

The structural question survives any finding against an individual director: what arrangement prevents the people who benefit from and implement decisions becoming sufficient authority to approve them without independent consent?

The surrounding payment sequence is exact: Philipp voted YES on A271 to release his Estima kickoff payment on 30 September 2026. The 45,000 USDC payment occurred on 3 October at 14:47:41 UTC. He created A272 at 15:02:16 UTC, 14 minutes 35 seconds later. His proposed removal case invoked another director’s interested vote. The accountability test must cover his own beneficiary approvals as well as those he criticizes.

Consequence

The attempted change would combine the pair’s financial and technical position with sufficient board votes. An independent third seat would remain present but lose its ability to require agreement.

Record details

The captured board remains four equal seats with a 75% threshold. A272’s failed status and the separate supervisor proposal are preserved in full.

Delegation discretion was exercised in an actual validator removal

The owner could change a validator’s economic position without a Phoenix board vote.

On 9 October 2026, Philipp’s hub-owner wallet removed Ceres from the eligible validator list and then rebalanced native delegations. The recorded rebalance moved 1,680,127.496132 LUNA, worth $76,889.82 at that time. Ceres’s net reduction was 662,828.893153 LUNA—$30,333.88. This is a concrete use of the owner’s allocation power. A validator’s delegated principal supports its commission income and default governance weight. Eligibility and rebalancing decisions can therefore change both its business position and its role in chain governance.

Delegation is a continuing economic dependency. It can be changed by the controller, so influence over a validator is wider than the ballot cast on any one proposal.

1219–24 October 2026

The missed handover was defended as personal judgment

Philipp’s explanation made his confidence in the board the condition for transferring powers that had been subject to published deadlines.

In forum reply #55, dated 19 October 2026, Philipp said the TLA contracts remained under ERIS administration because ERIS continued to develop, maintain and operate them. He also said concerns about the board made transfer inappropriate in the ERIS team’s judgment.

The 2024 terms specified one month for administration and three months for ownership. His reply identifies no approving decision, authority or date replacing those deadlines with his continuing assessment of the board. The central accountability demand is therefore exact: produce the authorization for retaining and exercising the powers after the stated handover.

A separate transfer proposal, Amplifier P9, was recorded as passed on 24 October. Its executable payload contains two administrator changes, covering the hub and CW20-ICS20. At the captured blocks, the hub still named the existing administrator. The payload leaves hub configuration ownership, Prop Gauge administration, Treasury administration and TLA registry ownership outside that transfer.

The history therefore ends with a precise control question, not a title or assurance: which powers have actually moved, to which decision structure, and under what enforceable approval rule? A complete handover needs those authorities enumerated and its execution verified.

Consequence

The published deadlines were displaced in practice by the administrator’s own judgment. Accountability requires the authorizing record, scrutiny of the uses made of those powers, and a completed transfer that removes the retained control routes.

Record details

P9’s status, two-message payload and contract administrators are recorded at the 24 October snapshot. Later changes can be compared against those preserved records.

The central accountability problem

The operator, beneficiary and decision-maker repeatedly overlap. Public funding builds the products; treasury capital and user deposits make them important; delegation and rewards make other participants dependent; retained keys preserve control over implementation. The proposed two-of-three board would then remove the need for consent from outside Philipp and MB’s pairing.

The public exchange and reported pressure show why these connections matter: the powers were invoked over criticism, community conduct and the structure of oversight. Each episode must be judged by the authority held, the demand made, the consequence invoked and the action actually taken.

The case for accountability

The record supports a serious concern about governance capture through overlapping financial, technical and voting authority. Its strongest basis is the documented structure and conduct: retained and used powers, beneficiary approvals, dependence on delegated stake, and an attempted reduction of independent consent.

The accountability question is how entrusted authority became a continuing source of personal discretion, financial benefit and power over participation. The retained keys, interested approvals, delegation leverage and proposed voting threshold must be examined together.

Who authorized departure from the handover terms, under what authority, and when? Who independently reviewed payments benefiting decision-makers? What enforceable arrangement prevents the financially aligned pair becoming sufficient authority alone?
